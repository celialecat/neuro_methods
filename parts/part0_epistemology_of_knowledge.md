# Part 0 — A Critical History of Epistemology: The Problem of Knowledge

> **Why this Part comes first.** Parts I–IX take as given a distinction — *knowledge about* a system
> versus *understanding of* it — and build machinery on top of it. But that distinction is not new; it
> is the latest sediment of a 2,400-year argument about what knowledge *is*, what separates it from
> mere true opinion, and whether "understanding" is a species of knowledge or a different epistemic good
> altogether. This Part reconstructs that argument **critically** — not as a museum tour but as a
> series of moves and counter-moves, each of which bequeaths a problem to the next. The payoff is that
> our central thesis turns out to be a *recovery*, in measurable form, of a distinction the tradition
> kept rediscovering and kept losing: between **propositional knowledge-that** (the object of classical
> epistemology) and **understanding-why / grasp of structure** (a holistic, often non-propositional,
> manipulation-apt good). The history is not background to the project. It *is* the project's first half.

A note on scope and method. "Epistemology" in the broadest sense covers every theory of knowledge;
this Part is selective and *thesis-driven*. We follow one thread — *what distinguishes knowing a fact
from understanding a thing* — and read each tradition for what it says about that thread, flagging
where the tradition's own commitments make the distinction visible and where they obscure it. We are
deliberately critical: every position below is stated with its strongest motivation **and** its most
damaging objection, because a literature review that only summarizes is itself a specimen of
*knowledge about* the field rather than *understanding of* it.

## 0.1 The ancient settlement: knowledge as justified true belief, and the first crack

**Plato (*Theaetetus*, *Meno*, *Republic*).** Western epistemology opens with a *negative* result. In
the *Theaetetus*, Socrates examines and rejects three definitions of knowledge (*epistēmē*):
knowledge as perception (refuted by the relativity and error of the senses), as true belief (refuted
because true belief can be lucky or rhetorically induced), and as true belief *with an account*
(*logos*) — the famous "JTB" germ — which the dialogue leaves *aporetic*, unable to say what kind of
account converts true belief into knowledge without circularity. The *Meno* supplies the canonical
motivation for adding justification: a true belief about the road to Larissa is as good a *guide* as
knowledge, but knowledge is more *stable* — it is "tied down" by "reasoning about the cause"
(*aitias logismos*). Two ideas are born here that run through the whole history: (i) knowledge is
true belief *plus* something that secures it; (ii) the securing factor is, paradigmatically, *grasp of
the cause/explanation*. The second idea is already an understanding-first intuition smuggled into an
analysis of knowledge.

**Critique.** Plato's own positive epistemology (the Forms, recollection, the divided line) ties
genuine *epistēmē* to acquaintance with timeless, necessary objects, and demotes the empirical world
to *doxa* (opinion). This buys stability at the price of making knowledge of the *changing, particular,
complex* systems we actually care about — radios, brains, networks — second-class by construction. The
problem the *Theaetetus* could not solve (what is the right *logos*?) is, recognizably, the problem
this whole project confronts: **what kind of account turns a correct prediction into understanding?**

**Aristotle (*Posterior Analytics*, *Nicomachean Ethics* VI).** Aristotle gives the first *systematic*
answer and, crucially, the first *typology* of epistemic states. Scientific knowledge proper
(*epistēmē*) is demonstrative: to know a thing *scientifically* is to know it *through its cause* and
to know that it *could not be otherwise* — knowledge of the "reasoned fact" (*to dioti*, the
*why*) as opposed to the mere "fact" (*to hoti*, the *that*). His doctrine of the four causes
(material, formal, efficient, final) is a theory of what an explanatory account must supply, and his
distinction between knowing *that* something is so and knowing *why* it is so is, almost verbatim, our
knowledge-about / understanding-of distinction. In the *Ethics* he separates *epistēmē* (demonstrative
science) from *technē* (productive craft-knowledge — knowing how to *make/repair*) and *phronēsis*
(practical wisdom — knowing how to *act* well). This tripartition anticipates a modern theme: that
**repair and design (Lazebnik's test) are a distinct epistemic competence** from propositional
science, and that an account can be rich in *epistēmē* yet poor in *technē*.

**Critique.** Aristotle's demand that science demonstrate from necessary first principles via syllogism
is too strong for the contingent, near-decomposable, statistically-described systems of Part V; almost
no real science meets it. But strip away the syllogistic packaging and the *content* survives
intact and is the backbone of this entire review: **understanding = grasp of the why, of the cause,
of why-it-could-not-easily-be-otherwise (invariance under intervention).** Aristotle is the first
interventionist in spirit, two millennia before Woodward and Pearl supply the formal "could not be
otherwise" (counterfactual/modal) machinery.

## 0.2 Rationalism vs. empiricism: where does the warrant come from?

The early-modern period reframes the question from *what is knowledge?* to *what is the source of its
warrant?* — and the two camps give answers that still structure the prediction/understanding debate.

**Rationalism (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz).** Descartes seeks an *Archimedean* point — the *cogito* —
and a method (clear and distinct ideas, deductive reconstruction) that would make knowledge
*indubitable* and, ideally, *generative*: from a few certain principles, derive the rest. Spinoza's
*Ethics* is the limiting case — the world presented *more geometrico*, each fact deduced from
definitions and axioms, so that to understand a thing is to see it *follow necessarily* from the nature
of substance (*sub specie aeternitatis*). Leibniz adds the *principle of sufficient reason* (nothing is
without a why) and the dream of a *characteristica universalis* + *calculus ratiocinator*: a formal
language in which disputes are settled by computation. This is the first articulation of an idea our
Part VI will need — that **understanding is compression to a generative core from which the phenomena
can be re-derived** — and Leibniz's "the simplest hypothesis covering the most phenomena" is a direct
ancestor of MDL and of Kitcher's unification.

**Critique.** Rationalism's generativity is exactly the right intuition about understanding (derive the
many from the few), but its certainty-seeking is a dead end: the systems we want to understand are not
deducible from self-evident axioms, and the demand for indubitability collapses (Descartes needs God
to underwrite clear-and-distinct ideas, an argument almost no one accepts). What survives is the
**ideal of a minimal generative model** — secularized later as algorithmic information and the
ε/ι-machine.

**Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume).** Locke grounds all ideas in experience (no innate principles; the
mind as *tabula rasa*) and, importantly for us, distinguishes *nominal* essences (our classificatory
constructs) from *real* essences (the unknown microstructure that actually explains a thing's
behavior) — conceding that we may have extensive knowledge-about a substance (its observable qualities)
while its real, explanatory essence stays hidden. This is Locke anticipating, in 1689, the gap between
a decoded representation and the mechanism that produces it. Hume then detonates the bomb the rest of
epistemology is still defusing: the **problem of induction** (no number of observed regularities
deductively warrants a prediction about the unobserved) and the **critique of causation** (we never
*observe* necessary connection, only constant conjunction + temporal succession + our *habit* of
expectation). Causation, for Hume, is a projection of the mind onto regularities.

**Critique and consequence.** Hume is the patron saint of this project's *negative* half. If all we
ever have is constant conjunction — Pearl's rung 1, the joint distribution — then **causal-mechanistic
understanding is not extractable from observation alone**, which is precisely Theorem T1 (the data
ceiling) restated in 18th-century terms. Hume's challenge forces a fork that defines everything after:
either (a) causation/mechanism is a useful fiction and science should aim only at prediction
(*instrumentalism*, the ancestor of "prediction is enough"), or (b) something *beyond* passive
observation — intervention, experiment, inductive bias, a priori structure — is needed to license
causal talk. This review takes fork (b) and makes the "something beyond" explicit and measurable
(intervention algebra, the variety bound). Hume's ghost is the reason understanding is not free.

## 0.3 Kant's synthesis: the knower contributes structure

**Kant (*Critique of Pure Reason*).** Kant's "Copernican revolution" answers Hume by relocating part of
the structure of knowledge *into the knower*. Causality, substance, space, and time are not read off
the world; they are *a priori* forms of intuition and categories of the understanding that the subject
*imposes* on the manifold of sensation to make experience possible at all. Hence *synthetic a priori*
knowledge: causation is necessary and universal *because* it is a precondition of any coherent
experience, not because it is observed. Knowledge is thus a *joint product* of given matter (sensation)
and contributed form (the categories), and we know only *phenomena* (things as structured by us), never
*noumena* (things in themselves).

**Critique and relevance.** Kant's specific list of categories and his claim that Euclidean space and
Newtonian causality are *a priori certain* did not survive non-Euclidean geometry and relativity. But
the architectural insight is one of the most important in this review: **the structure we find in a
system is partly a projection of the cognitive apparatus that finds it.** This is exactly the *reflexive
turn* of Part IX — the variety bound `V(A) ≥ C_ι`, the claim that the levels and hierarchies we
"discover" depend on the observer's capacity, and the warning that an "understanding horizon" may be set
as much by us as by the system. Kant is the first to say that an account of understanding must be an
account of the *understander*, not only of the understood. Where we depart from Kant: our "contributed
structure" is not fixed a priori but is the agent's finite, *upgradable* repertoire of interventions and
representations — instruments are variety amplifiers that extend the Kantian apparatus.

## 0.4 Positivism, conventionalism, and the dream of eliminating "understanding"

**Comte, Mach, and logical positivism (the Vienna Circle: Schlick, Carnap, Neurath; with Hempel as
fellow traveler).** The positivist tradition is the high-water mark of *anti-understanding* epistemology
— and is therefore the most important foil for this project. Auguste Comte's "positive" stage abjures
the search for hidden causes and final essences in favor of describing *laws* (constant relations among
observables). Ernst Mach pushes further: science is the *economical* description of sensations
(*Denkökonomie*); concepts like atoms and absolute space are admissible only as compact summaries of
observations, not as hidden mechanisms — *explanation reduces to economical description*. Mach's
"economy of thought" is, quite literally, an early statement of **understanding-as-compression** (Part
II), and his instrumentalism about atoms is the purest form of "prediction/description is all there
is."

The Vienna Circle then tries to *demarcate* meaningful science via the **verifiability criterion**
(meaning = method of verification) and to reconstruct theories as logical systems over an observation
language. Hempel's **Deductive-Nomological model** (explanation = deduction of the explanandum from
laws + initial conditions) is the culmination: to explain is to show the event was *to be expected*
given the laws — explanation and prediction become **structurally symmetric**.

**Critique — and why it matters most.** The positivist programme failed on its own terms, and the *way*
it failed is the conceptual core of this entire review:
- The verifiability criterion is self-refuting (it is not itself verifiable) and too strict (it
  excludes universally-quantified laws).
- The **symmetry thesis is false**, and its failure is the single most important result in the
  philosophy of explanation for our purposes. Counterexamples (Bromberger's flagpole: the shadow's
  length and the sun's elevation let you *deduce* — hence "D-N explain" — the pole's height, but the
  height does not *explain* the shadow's-cause runs one way; Salmon's barometer-and-storm; the man who
  avoids pregnancy by taking birth-control pills) show you can have **perfect deductive prediction with
  zero explanatory force, and vice versa**. *This is Theorem T2 (the clever-predictor gap) in its
  original philosophical habitat.* The asymmetry the D-N model cannot capture is **causal/explanatory
  directionality**, which is exactly what intervention recovers and observation does not.

So the positivist episode is not a dead end to be skipped; it is the **controlled experiment** that
proves the thesis. A movement of extraordinary rigor *tried* to reduce understanding to prediction +
economical description, and the reduction broke on the rock of explanatory asymmetry. Everything in
Parts III–VI is downstream of that break.

**Conventionalism (Poincaré, Duhem).** A parallel current: Poincaré argued some principles (the
geometry of space) are *conventions* chosen for convenience, not facts; Duhem argued that hypotheses
face experience only *in bundles* (the **Duhem–Quine** under-determination: a failed prediction never
tells you *which* assumption to blame). Under-determination is a permanent feature of our problem: it is
why prediction cannot uniquely fix mechanism (many models fit the data — the T2 gap again) and why
**intervention is needed to break the tie** (Part IV).

## 0.5 The post-positivist revolt: falsification, paradigms, research programmes, anarchy

**Popper.** Conceding Hume (induction is invalid), Popper relocates rationality from *confirmation* to
*falsification*: theories are never verified, only corroborated by surviving severe tests, and the mark
of science is *falsifiability*. Knowledge grows by conjecture and refutation; verisimilitude
(truth-likeness) is approached but never possessed. For us, Popper contributes the **severe-test**
ideal that Part VII operationalizes as benchmarks with known ground truth, and the insight that a model's
worth is set by the *risks it takes* (out-of-distribution, counterfactual, interventional predictions),
not by its accommodation of seen data.

**Critique.** Naive falsificationism is too brittle (the Duhem–Quine point: you can always save a theory
by adjusting auxiliaries; lone falsifications rarely kill theories) and descriptively false as history
(scientists do not abandon theories at the first anomaly). It also says little about *understanding* per
se — a falsified-but-not-yet-replaced theory can still be the most *understanding-conferring* one
available (Newtonian mechanics).

**Kuhn (*Structure of Scientific Revolutions*).** Knowledge is produced within *paradigms* — shared
exemplars, methods, and standards — punctuated by *revolutions* in which paradigms are replaced.
Crucially, competing paradigms are **incommensurable**: they carve the world differently, so that
"mass" means something different in Newton and Einstein, and there is no theory-neutral observation
language to adjudicate between them (observation is **theory-laden**). Kuhn matters here for two reasons.
First, theory-ladenness is the empirical correlate of Kant: *what you can even measure depends on the
conceptual scheme you bring* — a constraint on any "objective" criterion of understanding (Part IV must
face the charge that its dimensions are paradigm-relative). Second, incommensurability prefigures our
**indeterminacy theorem T3**: understanding is fixed only up to a choice of level/scheme (ι-machine
isomorphism), and rival "understandings" may be inter-translatable only with loss.

**Critique.** Kuhn's stronger relativist readings (no rational theory-choice, no progress toward truth)
are widely rejected and arguably self-undermining; the more defensible Kuhn is the one who shows that
*standards of explanation are partly internal to a practice* — which we accept, while insisting (with
the benchmarks) that on engineered systems there *is* a paradigm-independent ground truth against which
methods can be scored.

**Lakatos** rationalizes the Popper–Kuhn tension: science consists of **research programmes** with a
protected *hard core* and a *protective belt* of auxiliaries; programmes are *progressive* (predict
novel facts) or *degenerating* (only accommodate after the fact). This gives a *diachronic* criterion
that maps cleanly onto our framework: a method/programme earns understanding-credit by **novel,
risky, transferable** success (Part VI's transferability and depth/breadth), not by post-hoc fit.

**Feyerabend (*Against Method*)** denies any fixed scientific method ("anything goes"), citing the messy,
opportunistic history of real discovery. Read charitably (not as relativism but as *methodological
pluralism*), this is a healthy warning against our own project: there is no single algorithm for
understanding, and the **concrete method of `methods/concrete_method.md` is offered as a disciplined
default, not a universal recipe** — its steps are defeasible and domain-adapted.

## 0.6 Quine and the naturalistic turn: epistemology becomes a science

**Quine ("Two Dogmas of Empiricism", "Epistemology Naturalized").** Quine dynamites two pillars of
positivism: the **analytic/synthetic distinction** (there are no truths true *by meaning alone*,
insulated from experience) and **reductionism** (the idea that each statement has its own range of
confirming experiences). What replaces them is **confirmational holism** — "our statements about the
external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body" —
the "web of belief", revisable anywhere under recalcitrant experience. Then the radical move:
**epistemology should become a chapter of psychology/natural science**, studying how the "meager input"
of sensory stimulation yields the "torrential output" of theory, dropping the foundationalist project of
justifying science from outside.

**Relevance and critique.** Naturalized epistemology is the methodological license for *this entire
review*: we treat "what is understanding?" not as a purely a priori conceptual question but as one to be
answered partly by **looking at successful and failed scientific practice** (Jonas–Kording, Lazebnik) and
by **building measurable criteria and benchmarks** — epistemology as engineering. The standard objection
(Kim: a purely descriptive psychology of belief-formation cannot recover the *normative* "ought" of
justification) is real and shapes our stance: we do **not** abandon normativity (the dimensions of Part
VI are normative standards), but we *naturalize its test* (intervention, transfer, repair on systems with
known ground truth). Holism, meanwhile, is the philosophical root of under-determination and of why
faithfulness must be tested at the level of the *whole model's interventional behavior*, not statement by
statement.

## 0.7 Gettier and the analysis of knowledge: the degeneration of JTB

**Gettier (1963).** Three pages end the 2,000-year JTB consensus. Gettier exhibits cases of *justified
true belief that are not knowledge*: you infer a true conclusion from a justified but false premise, or
your justified belief is true by luck in a way that disconnects the justification from the truth-maker
(Smith's "the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket"). The diagnosis: **justification does
not guarantee the right kind of connection between belief and fact**; truth reached by luck is not
knowledge.

**The post-Gettier industry and its lesson.** Decades of repair attempts followed:
- **No-false-lemmas** (add: justification uses no false premise) — patched by fake-barn cases.
- **Causal theory** (Goldman 1967: the fact must *cause* the belief) — closer to our spirit (knowledge
  needs a causal tie to the world) but fails for abstract/mathematical knowledge and for deviant causal
  chains.
- **Reliabilism** (Goldman 1979: a belief is justified iff produced by a *reliable* process) — shifts
  from internal reasons to external truth-tracking; objections: the generality problem (how to type the
  process?), BonJour's clairvoyant, the new evil-demon problem.
- **Tracking / sensitivity & safety** (Nozick: S knows iff, were p false, S would not believe p; later
  *safety*: S would not easily believe p falsely) — counterfactual conditions that, note, are exactly
  **rung-3 (counterfactual) conditions on the believer**, mirroring our counterfactual-competence
  dimension applied to the *understander*.
- **Virtue epistemology** (Sosa, Zagzebski): knowledge is *apt* belief — true *because* competent, a
  cognitive achievement creditable to the agent's intellectual virtue. This reframes knowledge as a
  *success-through-ability*, which generalizes naturally to understanding-as-competence.
- **Knowledge-first** (Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*): *abandon the analysis*. Knowledge is a
  primitive, unanalyzable mental state (the most general factive one), and we should explain belief,
  justification, and evidence *in terms of* knowledge, not vice versa.

**Critique — and the pivot to understanding.** The honest verdict on the Gettier programme is that
**fifty years of analysis produced no agreed reductive definition of propositional knowledge**, and
Williamson's response — stop trying — is now widely respected. For our purposes the decisive observation
is sharper: *the entire post-Gettier debate is about **knowledge-that p** — discrete propositional
states* — and it is increasingly clear that **the epistemic good that matters for science is not
captured by any analysis of "S knows that p" at all.** A scientist can know thousands of true
propositions about a system (every tuning curve, every correlation) and not understand it; conversely,
understanding can survive minor falsehoods and idealizations (Elgin). The Gettier literature's
exhaustion is, read correctly, the negative result that *motivates the affirmative turn to a separate
epistemology of understanding* — to which we now come.

## 0.8 The epistemology of understanding: the distinction, finally thematized

For most of the 20th century, analytic epistemology treated *understanding* as either reducible to
knowledge of explanations or as a merely *psychological* (hence epistemically irrelevant) feeling — the
"aha." Since roughly 2000 a dedicated literature has rehabilitated understanding as a *distinct and
arguably superior* epistemic state. This is the part of the tradition closest to our thesis, and we read
it critically.

- **Zagzebski (2001), Kvanvig (2003, *The Value of Knowledge and the Pursuit of Understanding*).**
  Kvanvig argues understanding is *objectively distinct* from knowledge: it is **holistic** (it concerns
  how things *hang together* — the grasp of relations within a body of information, not isolated
  propositions), and it is **not threatened by Gettier-style luck** in the way knowledge is. You can
  understand a subject even if some of your beliefs about it were luckily true. This holism — *grasp of
  structure/coherence-making relations* — is precisely the "hierarchical, causal-mechanistic structure"
  our framework demands, and Kvanvig's luck-insensitivity foreshadows why understanding is graded and
  robust rather than all-or-nothing.
- **Elgin (2007, 2017, *True Enough*).** The most radical and, for us, most important move: understanding
  is **not factive** in the strict sense — it tolerates, and often *requires*, *felicitous falsehoods*:
  idealizations (frictionless planes, point masses, ideal gases, infinite populations) that are literally
  false yet *understanding-conferring*. Science advances by *models that are true enough for the
  cognitive purpose*. This is the philosophical charter for Part VI's treatment of **mechanistic
  faithfulness as graded** and for the whole apparatus of levels/coarse-graining: the right model is not
  the most *accurate* but the one that is *faithful at the chosen level* and *closed under the
  interventions of interest* (the ι-machine is an idealization, deliberately).
- **Pritchard (2009, 2014).** Distinguishes *understanding-why* and *objectual* understanding from
  *propositional knowledge*, and argues understanding has a distinctive **luck-incompatibility profile**
  and requires a *grasp* of explanatory connections (not just a true belief in an explanation). Grasp is
  partly an *ability* — to draw inferences, answer what-ifs — which is our **counterfactual competence**.
- **Grimm (2006, 2011).** Understanding as a kind of **"grasping"** of the modal/structural relations in a
  system — knowing not just *that* the variables are related but *how* changes in one would propagate to
  others. This is an explicitly **interventionist/modal** account of understanding: to understand is to
  command the dependency structure well enough to answer "what would happen if…" — i.e., to possess (a
  model of) the ι-machine.
- **de Regt (2017, *Understanding Scientific Understanding*).** A *pragmatic-contextual* theory:
  scientific understanding = the ability to *use* a theory, where a theory is intelligible to scientists
  with the relevant skills if they can recognize qualitatively characteristic consequences *without
  exact calculation*. Understanding is thus a **skill-laden, agent-relative** achievement (intelligibility
  is not a property of theories alone but of theory-plus-scientist). This is the most direct philosophical
  source for our **reflexive turn**: intelligibility depends on the *understander's* repertoire — de Regt
  is Ashby's requisite variety in the philosophy of science.
- **Khalifa (2017, *Understanding, Explanation, and Scientific Knowledge*).** The principal *dissent*,
  which intellectual honesty requires we state in full force: Khalifa argues understanding is **not**
  *sui generis* but reduces to **knowledge of explanations** — to understand why p is, roughly, to know a
  correct explanation of p (and to grasp it = to have explanatory knowledge that meets ordinary
  epistemic standards). On this *explanatory-knowledge* view, there is no extra ingredient beyond good
  explanation plus its (knowledge-grade) possession, and the apparent specialness of understanding is the
  specialness of *explanation*.

**Our position, stated critically.** We side with the *distinctness* camp (Kvanvig/Elgin/Pritchard/
Grimm/de Regt) on the substance, but we *agree with Khalifa's instinct* that the distinctness must not be
mystical — it must cash out in something assessable. That is exactly what this project does: it accepts
that understanding is *grasp of (interventionally validated, level-appropriate) explanatory structure*
(close to Khalifa) **but** insists, against a purely propositional reading, that the relevant grasp is
(i) **holistic** (Kvanvig), (ii) **manipulation/ability-apt** (Grimm, de Regt — answer counterfactuals,
predict OOD, repair, redesign), (iii) **graded and idealization-tolerant** (Elgin), and (iv) **measurable**
along the dimensions of Part VI. The dispute between Khalifa and the distinctness theorists is, in our
frame, *dissolved by operationalization*: whether you call understanding "explanatory knowledge plus
grasp" or "a distinct good", the **test** is the same — does the agent possess a model that *commutes
with intervention* on the target system? Knowledge-about fails this test no matter how complete; that is
the through-line from Plato's *aitias logismos* to Theorem T1.

## 0.9 Two further threads the propositional tradition under-weights

**Knowing-how vs. knowing-that (Ryle); tacit knowledge (Polanyi).** Gilbert Ryle (*The Concept of Mind*,
1949) argued that **knowing-how** (riding a bike, diagnosing a fault) is not reducible to knowing-that
(a set of propositions) — intelligent performance is not the application of antecedently-known rules
(on pain of regress). Michael Polanyi (*Personal Knowledge*, 1958; *The Tacit Dimension*, 1966) made the
companion point: "**we know more than we can tell**"; skilled scientific judgment rests on *tacit*,
non-articulable knowledge. These matter to our project in two ways. (i) **Repair and redesign** —
Lazebnik's and our own criteria — are *knowing-how* competences; a framework that scored only
propositional outputs would miss them, which is why Part IV includes *intervene-optimally / repair /
redesign* as criteria and Part VI includes *transferability* and *robustness*. (ii) The tacit dimension
is a caution about **operationalization**: some genuine understanding may resist explicit articulation,
so our measurable dimensions should be read as *necessary public correlates* of understanding, not as a
claim that understanding *is nothing but* its measurable signatures. (The debate has a live opponent —
Stanley & Williamson (2001) argue knowing-how *is* a species of propositional knowledge — which keeps the
issue honest rather than settled.)

**French *épistémologie historique* (Bachelard, Canguilhem, Foucault).** A tradition the Anglophone
analysis ignores at its peril, and one strikingly aligned with this project's emphasis on the *historical
and constructive* nature of intelligibility.
- **Gaston Bachelard** (*La formation de l'esprit scientifique*, 1938): scientific knowledge advances by
  *rupture* with everyday perception and intuition, overcoming **epistemological obstacles** (*obstacles
  épistémologiques*) — entrenched images and habits (substantialist, animist, "common-sense" intuitions)
  that *block* understanding. Crucially, Bachelard holds that the obstacle is *internal to the knowing
  mind*, not just a lack of data. This is a direct ancestor of our claim that the barrier to understanding
  complex systems is often **conceptual/representational, not evidential** (T1: more data does not lift the
  ceiling; you need new interventions and new concepts — a *rupture*). His "the real is never 'what one
  might believe' but always what one ought to have thought" is naturalized epistemology with a normative
  edge.
- **Georges Canguilhem** (*Le normal et le pathologique*, 1943): concepts in the life sciences (normal,
  pathological, function, regulation) have histories and are *constituted*, not given; understanding a
  biological function is inseparable from the normative/teleological framing that individuates it. This
  bears directly on neuroscience: *what counts as "the function" of a circuit* is partly a conceptual
  decision (Part VIII), and "function localization" inherits all the difficulties Canguilhem identified.
- **Michel Foucault** (*Les mots et les choses*, 1966; *L'archéologie du savoir*, 1969): each era has an
  *épistémè* — a historical a priori fixing what can count as a possible object of knowledge and a valid
  statement. This is Kuhn's paradigm radicalized to the level of the conditions of knowledge itself, and a
  strong statement of the **observer-relativity** our reflexive turn (Part IX) treats formally. We borrow
  the spirit (the knowable is historically/structurally conditioned) while resisting the strongest
  relativism (on engineered benchmarks there is a construction-independent ground truth — indeed, by the
  **Genesis Principle**, the *construction history is the source of intelligibility*, which turns
  Foucault's insight into a testable claim about systems rather than only about discourses).

## 0.10 Synthesis: what the history bequeaths to this project

Reading the tradition for our one thread, a small number of robust results emerge — each a load-bearing
commitment of Parts I–IX, and each *earned*, not assumed:

1. **The why/that distinction is ancient and ineliminable** (Aristotle's *dioti*/*hoti*). Knowledge of
   the *that* (regularities, correlations, decodings) does not amount to knowledge of the *why* (causes,
   mechanism). *Knowledge-about / understanding-of is the modern, measurable form of this distinction.*
2. **Causal-mechanistic understanding is not extractable from passive observation** (Hume's induction and
   causation critiques; Duhem–Quine under-determination). Something beyond data is required: intervention,
   a priori structure, or inductive bias. *This is Theorem T1.*
3. **Prediction and explanation come apart** (the failure of the positivist symmetry thesis;
   Bromberger/Salmon asymmetries). A perfect predictor can be explanatorily empty. *This is Theorem T2.*
4. **The structure we find is partly contributed by the knower** (Kant; theory-ladenness in Kuhn;
   de Regt's intelligibility; Foucault's épistémè). An account of understanding must include the
   *understander*. *This is the reflexive turn / variety bound of Part IX.*
5. **Understanding is determined only up to a choice of scheme/level** (Kuhnian incommensurability;
   Elgin's idealization; conventionalism). *This is the indeterminacy theorem T3 (understanding up to
   ι-machine isomorphism).*
6. **Understanding is holistic, graded, ability-apt, and idealization-tolerant** — not a discrete
   propositional state (Kvanvig, Pritchard, Grimm, de Regt, Elgin; Ryle/Polanyi on knowing-how and tacit
   knowledge). *This licenses the graded, multi-dimensional definition of Part VI and the criteria
   (repair, redesign, counterfactuals, transfer) of Part IV.*
7. **The exhaustion of the analysis of propositional knowledge** (Gettier → Williamson) is the negative
   result that *clears the ground* for an affirmative, operational theory of understanding — which is what
   this review supplies.

The verdict is bracing and, we think, original in its framing: **the long history of epistemology is in
large part the history of trying, and failing, to reduce understanding to a well-behaved species of
propositional knowledge** — to JTB, to verified observation-statements, to D-N deductions, to a Gettier-
proof analysis. Each failure failed *in the same place*: at the point where *grasp of why-structure that
survives intervention* outruns *secured true belief that p*. Our contribution is to stop treating that
gap as a residue to be analyzed away and start treating it as the **primary object**, to give it
coordinates `(KA, UO)`, a minimal model (the ι-machine), and a test (does the model commute with
intervention?). The history did not merely lead here; properly read, **it has been pointing here the
whole time.**
